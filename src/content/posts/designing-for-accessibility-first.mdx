Over a billion people worldwide live with some form of disability. When we build inaccessible products, we're not just failing a compliance audit — we're actively excluding a significant portion of our potential users.

But here's the more compelling argument: **accessible design is better design**. The constraints that make a product work for people with disabilities almost always make it work better for everyone.

## The Business Case

Accessibility is often framed as a legal requirement or a moral obligation. Both are true. But the business case is equally strong:

- The global disability market controls over $13 trillion in disposable income
- Accessible sites perform better in search engines — screen-reader-friendly markup is also SEO-friendly markup
- Captions benefit not just deaf users but anyone watching video in a noisy environment
- High contrast modes help users in bright sunlight, not just those with low vision

When Microsoft redesigned Xbox controllers for accessibility, the adaptive controller became one of their most praised products — and opened up the gaming market to millions of new customers.

## The Four Principles (POUR)

WCAG 2.1 organizes accessibility requirements around four principles. Every requirement flows from one of these:

### Perceivable
Information must be presentable in ways users can perceive. This means:
- Images have meaningful `alt` text
- Videos have captions and transcripts
- Color is never the *only* way to convey information
- Text has sufficient contrast against its background

### Operable
Users must be able to operate the interface. This means:
- All functionality is available via keyboard
- No content flashes more than three times per second (seizure risk)
- Users have enough time to read and interact with content
- Navigation is logical and consistent

### Understandable
Information and UI must be understandable. This means:
- Language is declared in HTML (`lang` attribute)
- Error messages are specific and helpful
- Labels describe their form fields accurately
- Navigation behaves predictably

### Robust
Content must be robust enough to work with assistive technologies. This means:
- Valid, semantic HTML
- ARIA attributes used correctly (or not at all — misused ARIA is worse than none)
- Name, role, and value exposed for all UI components

## Practical Wins You Can Ship Today

You don't need to overhaul your entire codebase. Start here:

**1. Audit your color contrast.** Use the browser DevTools accessibility panel or tools like Colour Contrast Analyser. Aim for a 4.5:1 ratio for body text, 3:1 for large text.

**2. Add `alt` text that means something.** "image.jpg" is not alt text. Describe what the image conveys, not what it depicts. A chart showing revenue growth should say "Bar chart showing 40% revenue growth from Q1 to Q4 2023" — not "chart."

**3. Test keyboard navigation.** Open your site. Put your mouse away. Can you reach every interactive element with Tab? Can you activate it with Enter or Space? Can you see what's focused?

**4. Use semantic HTML.** A `<button>` is better than a `<div onClick>`. A `<nav>` is better than a `<div class="nav">`. Semantic HTML gives assistive technologies the context they need for free.

**5. Don't suppress focus styles.** `outline: none` without a replacement is the single most common accessibility mistake. If the default focus ring is ugly, design a better one — don't remove it.

## The Shift in Mindset

Accessibility-first means considering the full spectrum of human diversity before you open your design tool, not after you've shipped. It means asking "how does a keyboard user complete this flow?" at the same time you ask "how does a mouse user complete this flow?"

The teams that do this consistently don't just check compliance boxes — they build products that are genuinely easier to use, for everyone.

That's not a constraint. That's craftsmanship.
